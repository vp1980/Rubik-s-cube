Before you start. This is not a test and there is no pass mark. It is a set of puzzles designed to find out what you already know, so that everything we do afterwards is pitched at the right level for you.

Some of these will be easy. Some will cover things you have not been taught yet — that is deliberate, and leaving one blank tells us something useful. Do not worry about the ones you cannot do. Just show your working where you can, so we can see how you were thinking.

Only Part A is against the clock. Everything else you can take at your own pace.

Part C · EnglishReading

About 16 minutes · read the passage twice before you start answering

The Lamp Room

from a short story

Nobody had told Martha the lamp would be so loud. She had imagined a lighthouse as a silent thing — a finger of white standing patiently in the dark — but the mechanism above her head clicked and sighed and turned like something breathing, and the whole tower seemed to lean into each gust off the water.

Her grandfather moved around the lamp room without hurrying. Forty-one years, he had said at supper, and not one night dark. He said it the way other people mention the weather, which Martha had begun to understand was not modesty at all but something closer to the opposite: a fact so large he had stopped needing to decorate it.

“Put your hand here,” he said.

She pressed her palm to the brass housing. It was warm — warmer than she expected, warm as an animal — and beneath the warmth she could feel the turning, steady and unhurried. Three seconds, and then three seconds, and then three seconds again.

“Every lighthouse has its own rhythm,” he said. “Ours is three. Down the coast at Ardmore, it’s four and a pause. A captain out there in the black can’t see the rock, can’t see the shore, can’t see us. But he counts. And if he counts to three, he knows exactly where he is in the world.”

Martha looked out at the water, which was not really water at this hour so much as an absence — a great moving nothing pressed up against the glass. Somewhere in it, she supposed, someone was counting.

She thought about that for a long time afterwards. Long after the tower was automated, long after her grandfather had gone down the stairs for the last time. It seemed to her a rare thing, to be so precisely and reliably yourself that a stranger could find their way home by it.

  1. How many years had Martha’s grandfather worked at the lighthouse?
  2. What is the rhythm of the lighthouse at Ardmore?
  3. In paragraph 2, the writer says her grandfather had “stopped needing to decorate” the fact. What does decorate mean here?
  4. Find and copy a word from the passage that means something that is not there.
  5. Martha thinks her grandfather’s tone “was not modesty at all but something closer to the opposite”. What does she mean by this?
  6. How do we know the visit takes place at night? Give two pieces of evidence from the passage.
  7. The writer describes the brass housing as “warm as an animal”.
    (a) What is this kind of language called?
    (b) Why do you think the writer chose it?
  8. In your own words, what has Martha understood by the end of the passage?
