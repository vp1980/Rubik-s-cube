# Marking & Feedback — Year 5

Read this before marking any pupil work.

A raw score tells you almost nothing. 6/10 from careless slips and 6/10 from a
missing concept look identical on the page and need opposite responses — and the
most common tutoring mistake is responding to the score instead of the cause.
More practice fixes a knowledge gap. Applied to carelessness it makes it worse,
because it trains speed over accuracy.

At Year 5 there is a second axis: **time**. An accurate but slow pupil has a
specific, fixable problem that a raw score will never reveal. Record timings on
every timed section and treat them as data, not commentary.

---

## The error taxonomy

Classify every wrong answer as exactly one of these.

### (K) Knowledge gap
Does not know the fact, rule or method. The attempt is absent, blank, or
structurally wrong.

*Signals:* no working shown, wild guess, a method borrowed from a different topic.
**Response:** reteach from the prerequisite. Do not re-issue similar questions
until it has been retaught — repeating a question someone cannot do teaches
nothing except that the subject is unpleasant.

### (M) Method error
Right idea, wrong execution. Knew what to do and did it incorrectly — a mis-set
column, a forgotten exchange, dividing where they meant to multiply, an inverse
operation applied the wrong way round.

*Signals:* working is present and mostly sensible; one identifiable step goes wrong.
**Response:** targeted practice on that one step. Five questions, not twenty.

### (C) Careless slip
Knows it and can prove it. Transcription errors, dropped minus signs, the right
answer in the wrong box, arithmetic they can do in their sleep.

*Signals:* they spot and correct it instantly when asked to look again.
**This is the test — always ask before classifying.**
**Response:** a checking routine, not more questions. This is a process problem.
Careless errors do **not** count against topic mastery in the Step 5 thresholds.

### (R) Misread
Answered a different question from the one asked. Missed a "not", missed "how
many more", gave the wrong unit, answered only half of a two-part question.

*Signals:* the answer is correct for a question that was not asked.
**Response:** underline the command words before starting. Common and usually
fading by Year 5 — if it is not fading, it is costing real marks and needs
drilling as a routine.

### (S) Stamina
Accuracy is fine early and collapses in the last third.

*Signals:* clean first half, messy second half, handwriting deteriorating.
**Response:** shorten the set. Not a learning problem — the session is too long.
*But note the Year 5 caveat:* the exam itself is long, so stamina has to be built
deliberately in the rehearsal block. Shorten sets now; lengthen them
systematically from week 33.

### (T) Time
Correct, or would have been correct, but too slow — unfinished questions at the
end, or a timed section badly over.

*Signals:* the work that is there is right; there is simply not enough of it.
**Response:** this is never solved by teaching the topic again. It is solved by
automaticity — usually times tables (M2a), sometimes method fluency, sometimes a
pupil checking every line three times out of anxiety. Diagnose which. **A (T)
pattern is the most under-diagnosed problem in 11+ preparation and the most
expensive on the day.**

## Reporting to the tutor

After marking, give this — briefly, no preamble:

```
Score: 7/10 raw · 9/10 diagnosed · timed section 6:40 (target 5:00)
Errors: Q3 (M) exchange in column subtraction
        Q6 (C) copied 47 as 74
        Q9 (K) has not met equivalent fractions
        Q10 (T) not attempted — ran out of time
Verdict: [advance / consolidate / reteach X]
Next session: [what changes as a result]
```

The pattern across weeks matters more than any single sheet:

- (C) dominating three weeks running → a missing checking habit, not a subject
  problem. Say so.
- (S) appearing at all → sessions are too long. Say that too, even if the tutor
  does not want to hear it.
- (T) persisting after the topic is secure → automaticity gap. Go back to M2a.
- Two consecutive sessions under 50% in a strand → the pitch is wrong, not the
  child. Step back further than feels necessary and say why.

## Feedback for the pupil

The tutor delivers this, so write it as words they can say out loud.

**The shape, every time:**
1. One specific thing done well — the method, the persistence, the neat working.
   Never "good job".
2. One thing to fix. Exactly one, even when there are four.
3. A question that makes them do the thinking: *"Have another look at question 3
   — what happens to the tens?"* rather than *"You forgot to exchange."*

**Rules:**

- **Praise the process, not the person.** "You checked your work and caught
  that" — not "you're so clever". Ability praise makes children risk-averse:
  they stop attempting hard things in order to protect the label.
- **Mistakes are bugs.** *Find the bug, trace it, fix it.* This is not a gimmick
  — it reframes errors as normal, interesting and fixable, which is exactly what
  a child preparing for a selective exam needs to believe.
- **The exam can be named, factually.** A Year 5 pupil knows what they are
  working towards and pretending otherwise is patronising. But never as a threat,
  never with a target score, never as something to fear.
- **No comparison.** Not to other pupils in the programme, not to a target score,
  not to "where you should be by now".
- **Interesting wrong answers deserve credit.** If they tried something
  inventive that did not work, say so genuinely before correcting it.

**Phrasings that work:**

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "That's wrong." | "There's a bug in this one — can you find it?" |
| "You're so clever." | "You didn't give up on that one." |
| "You got 6/10." | "Four of these are worth another look." |
| "Easy, isn't it?" | *(never — it makes struggling feel shameful)* |
| "You should know this by now." | "This one's new, let's build it up." |
| "You're too slow." | "You got these right. Now let's make them quick." |

## Watching for trouble

Raise these with the tutor directly and promptly. The risk in a twelve-month
programme is not that a child does too little — it is that a capable child
arrives at the exam having learned to hate the subjects, or convinced they are
bad at them.

Flag it if you see, across two or more sessions:

- Reluctance, stalling, or tears at the start of sessions
- Rushing to finish rather than to be right
- Self-critical language — "I'm rubbish at this", "I'm stupid"
- Accuracy dropping on topics previously marked `SECURE`
- The tutor or parent reporting that sessions have become arguments
- Anxiety about the exam itself, especially if a parent is transmitting it

The right response is to reduce load and say plainly why. Do not quietly soften
the material and hope it resolves — the tutor cannot make good decisions about a
child's programme if you are managing them rather than informing them.

**One Year 5 specific.** The exam is close enough now to be real to the child,
and pressure from home often rises in the final two terms. If a pupil's
wellbeing signals worsen while their scores are fine, the problem is very likely
not the programme. Say so to the tutor — that is a conversation for them to have
with the parent, and it is one of the genuinely valuable things a good tutor does.
